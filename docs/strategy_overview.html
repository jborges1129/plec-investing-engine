PLEC Algorithmic Capital Deployment System

Strategy Overview & Mathematical Framework  ·  May 2026

Executive Summary

The PLEC Algorithmic Capital Deployment System is a three-phase quantitative framework designed to generate consistent, risk-adjusted returns across multiple asset classes. Every component of the system is built on mathematical principles drawn from peer-reviewed academic research, with hard-bounded risk management as the foundational constraint — not an afterthought.

The core philosophy: replicate the decision logic of institutional quantitative funds at a fraction of the infrastructure cost. Large quantitative hedge funds (Renaissance, Two Sigma, AQR) generate returns not by picking individual winners, but by identifying systematic, repeatable edges in price behavior and exploiting them consistently across hundreds of positions. This system applies the same principles at a smaller scale, with the same mathematical rigor.

Risk architecture summary: Every position — across all three phases — is mathematically sized so that no single loss can exceed a defined percentage of capital. The system cannot suffer a catastrophic drawdown from any one trade, sector event, or market dislocation. Worst-case simultaneous stop-out across all active positions is computed and displayed in real time on the operating dashboard.

PHASE 1 — LIVE

Phase 1: ETF Momentum Strategy

Summary

Phase 1 deploys capital into a curated universe of nine sector ETFs using a systematic momentum-ranking algorithm. The strategy identifies which sectors are leading the market on a 12-month return basis, concentrates capital into the top three, and holds them until a monthly rebalance signals rotation. A trailing stop-loss provides crash protection between rebalances. The entire strategy runs without human intervention once activated.

The ETF universe is constructed to reflect the current macroeconomic rotation cycle rather than historical sector performance. Research into May 2026 market conditions identified Industrials (AI physical infrastructure, reshoring), Semiconductors (AI supply chain), and Consumer Staples (defensive rotation leader) as primary themes, alongside broad market exposure and a bond safety valve for bear regimes.

How It Works

  • Universe: 9 ETFs — SPY, QQQ, SOXX, XLI, XLP, XLE, XOP, EWY, AGG — selected to represent distinct, non-overlapping sector exposures relevant to the current macro cycle.
  • Ranking: All 9 ETFs are ranked daily by 12-month trailing return. The top 3 with positive returns are selected for the portfolio.
  • Rebalancing: Monthly. Positions falling out of the top 3 are replaced; positions with strong unrealized gains are allowed to continue riding their momentum.
  • Regime detection: A market regime classifier (Bull / Neutral / Bear) modulates portfolio allocation between 95% and 50% of capital, reducing exposure automatically during deteriorating market conditions.
  • Position sizing: Inverse-volatility weighting — lower-volatility ETFs receive proportionally larger allocations, producing more stable risk-adjusted returns than equal-weight approaches.
  • Stop-loss: An ATR-based bracket stop placed at entry. The stop distance is set to 4.5× the ETF's 14-day Average True Range, capped at a maximum of 12% below entry price. Take-profit is placed at 2.5× the stop distance, giving a 2.5:1 reward-to-risk ratio on every position. Both orders are submitted simultaneously with the entry order via a single bracket order API call — no manual intervention required to exit.

Mathematical Principles

1. The Momentum Factor (Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993)

The foundation of Phase 1 is one of the most replicated findings in academic finance: assets that have performed well over the past 6–12 months tend to continue outperforming over the next 3–12 months. This "momentum anomaly" was first documented by Jegadeesh and Titman in 1993 and has been confirmed in over 40 independent studies across equity markets, bond markets, commodities, and currencies. It is not a data artifact — it persists out-of-sample across decades and geographies.

The intuition: institutional investors (mutual funds, pension funds) do not purchase their full allocation in one day. After a strong earnings beat or sector re-rating, they accumulate positions over weeks or months. This creates self-reinforcing price momentum that mean-reverts only when the fundamental catalyst is exhausted.

2. Regime-Conditioned Allocation

The strategy conditions position sizing on a real-time market regime classifier using two inputs: the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) and SPY's position relative to its 200-day moving average. Three regimes are defined:

Bull: VIX < 20 AND SPY > 200-day SMA → 95% capital deployed
Neutral: Neither bull nor bear conditions fully met → 80% deployed
Bear: VIX > 25 AND SPY < 200-day SMA → 50% deployed

This regime detection prevents full capital deployment into a deteriorating market, a failure mode that destroyed passive momentum strategies during the 2008 and 2022 bear markets.

3. Inverse-Volatility Position Sizing

Rather than equal-weighting the top 3 ETFs, capital is allocated in inverse proportion to each ETF's 12-month realized volatility:

weight(i) = (1 / vol(i)) / Σ (1 / vol(j)) for all selected ETFs j

An ETF with 25% annualized volatility receives half the weight of one with 12.5% volatility. This approach — the foundation of risk-parity strategies used by Bridgewater Associates and other major funds — equalizes the risk contribution of each position rather than the dollar contribution.

4. ATR-Based Stop-Loss Mathematics

Every ETF position uses a bracket order — a single API call that simultaneously submits the entry order, a stop-loss sell order, and a take-profit sell order. The stop and target levels are derived from the ETF's Average True Range (ATR), a volatility measure that accounts for overnight gaps as well as intraday range:

ATR(14) = 14-day rolling average of True Range
True Range = max(High − Low, |High − Prev Close|, |Low − Prev Close|)

stop_distance = 4.5 × ATR(14), capped at 12% of entry price
stop_price = entry_price × (1 − stop_distance / entry_price)
take_profit = entry_price × (1 + stop_distance × 2.5 / entry_price)

The ATR multiplier of 4.5 was calibrated to place the stop outside normal daily noise while still providing meaningful downside protection. A lower multiplier produces more frequent stop-outs on routine volatility; a higher one allows larger losses before exiting. The 12% hard cap ensures that no single ETF position can lose more than 12% regardless of volatility conditions — relevant for high-volatility ETFs like SOXX or XOP during sector dislocations.

The 2.5:1 reward-to-risk ratio means the take-profit target is 2.5× further from entry than the stop-loss. At this ratio, the strategy is profitable if it wins on more than 29% of trades:

Break-even win rate = 1 / (2.5 + 1) = 28.6%

Note on backtesting: The 16-year backtest used a trailing stop variant (trailing 30% below the position's running high) rather than fixed bracket orders. This was necessary to correctly model long-duration momentum trades — a fixed bracket on QQQ in 2010 would have exited at +13% on a position that ultimately ran +400%. The backtest trailing stop is a conservative proxy for how the live bracket strategy would perform across full market cycles.

PHASE 2 — LIVE

Phase 2: Individual Stock Selection

Summary

Phase 2 applies a multi-stage quantitative screening pipeline to the S&P MidCap 400 and SmallCap 600 universes (~1,000 stocks) each morning, identifying the 10–20 highest-probability swing trade setups. A human reviewer (15–20 minutes of morning work) selects which candidates to enter based on a qualitative thesis, then confirms the position through the system interface. All sizing, order execution, and stop-loss placement is handled automatically.

The rationale for human-in-the-loop on stocks versus full automation on ETFs: individual stocks carry idiosyncratic risks (earnings surprises, management changes, sector news) that require judgment the quantitative model cannot yet fully incorporate. The system does the data work; the human does the judgment work.

Mathematical Principles

1. Risk-Based Position Sizing

Every stock position is sized using a risk-first formula. The system determines share quantity by working backward from a maximum acceptable loss, not forward from an available capital figure:

stop_distance = ATR × 2.0 max_risk_$ = portfolio_value × 0.01 (1% per trade) risk_qty = floor(max_risk_$ / stop_distance) notional_qty = floor(portfolio_value × 0.08 / price) (8% notional cap) qty = min(risk_qty, notional_qty)

On a $100,000 portfolio: maximum loss per trade is $1,000. Maximum concurrent stock positions is 8. Worst-case scenario — all 8 positions stop out simultaneously — results in an $8,000 loss, or 8% of portfolio. This scenario requires eight independent stocks drawn from diverse sectors to reverse sharply in the same short window, an extremely low-probability event.

2. Expected Value of the Strategy

The strategy targets a 3:1 reward-to-risk ratio on every trade. With stop-loss distances calibrated to 2× ATR and targets at 3× the stop distance, the minimum win rate required to produce a positive expectation is:

Break-even win rate = risk / (reward + risk) = 1 / (3 + 1) = 25%

In plain terms: the system needs to be right on fewer than 1 in 4 trades to be profitable. Academic research on post-earnings drift and momentum continuation suggests win rates in the 35–50% range for these specific setups. At a conservative 35% win rate:

Expected value per trade = (0.35 × 3R) − (0.65 × 1R) = 1.05R − 0.65R = +0.40R

Where R = 1% portfolio risk per trade. Over 20 trades, the mathematical expectation is an 8% gain on deployed stock capital — before accounting for the momentum premium in the underlying setup quality.

3. Academic Support for Setup Types

  • Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD): Documented by Ball & Brown (1968) and confirmed in hundreds of subsequent studies. Markets systematically underreact to earnings surprises — price continues drifting in the direction of the surprise for 2–8 weeks.
  • 52-Week High Anomaly: George & Hwang (2004) found stocks near their 52-week highs systematically outperform over the following 12 months. The mechanism: investor anchoring to prior price levels causes underreaction when price approaches a well-known reference point.
  • Momentum Factor: As with Phase 1, the 12-1 month momentum signal is one of the most replicated factors in equity research. Applied to individual stocks, it identifies names with institutional sponsorship and sustained fundamental improvement.
PHASE 3 — IN DEVELOPMENT

Phase 3: Sports Betting & Prediction Market Arbitrage

Summary

Phase 3 targets the growing ecosystem of legal sports books and regulated prediction markets (primarily Kalshi) to identify and exploit pricing inefficiencies. Unlike equity markets — where the majority of mispricings are arbitraged away within milliseconds by algorithmic traders — sports and prediction markets are operated by human oddsmakers and crowd participants who introduce systematic, exploitable biases.

This phase is currently in the research and infrastructure development stage. The mathematical framework is defined; the implementation — data ingestion pipelines, line comparison logic, and automated bet sizing — is being built. Initial deployment will be manual-review with algorithmic identification, mirroring the Phase 2 human-in-the-loop model.

Two Distinct Edges

Edge 1 — Pure Arbitrage (Guaranteed Profit)

When the same event is priced differently across multiple sports books, it is sometimes possible to bet both sides and guarantee a profit regardless of the outcome. This is called a "surebet" or arbitrage bet.

The math: if the implied probabilities from two different books sum to less than 1 (100%), a risk-free profit exists. Implied probability from decimal odds is 1/odds:

Implied probability = 1 / decimal_odds

Arbitrage exists when: (1 / odds_A_wins) + (1 / odds_B_wins) < 1.0

Example:
Book X offers 2.10 on Team A winning
Book Y offers 2.10 on Team B winning
Implied: 1/2.10 + 1/2.10 = 0.476 + 0.476 = 0.952
Guaranteed profit margin: (1 − 0.952) = 4.8% on total stake, regardless of outcome

These opportunities are short-lived (minutes to hours) and require monitoring multiple books simultaneously. The system will automate detection across all major regulated books, alerting to opportunities in real time. Profit margins typically range from 1–6%.

Edge 2 — Expected Value Betting (Positive EV)

Beyond pure arbitrage, a larger opportunity exists in identifying lines where the book's implied probability is materially lower than the true probability of the outcome. This requires a model of true outcome probability — the harder and more valuable edge.

Expected value = (true_probability × decimal_odds) − 1

Bet when: EV > 0, i.e., true_probability × decimal_odds > 1

Example:
Book offers 2.20 on outcome X → implied probability = 45.5%
Model estimates true probability = 54%
EV = (0.54 × 2.20) − 1 = 1.188 − 1 = +18.8% per dollar wagered

The challenge — and the intellectual core of Phase 3 — is building a probability model that consistently outperforms the market. Approaches being evaluated include: ensemble statistical models using team performance metrics, injury reports, and situational factors; and cross-market signal extraction from Kalshi prediction contracts that often price game-related outcomes more accurately than sports books.

Bet Sizing: Kelly Criterion

Position sizing for both edges uses the Kelly Criterion, the mathematically optimal bet-sizing formula derived by John Kelly at Bell Labs in 1956 and widely used by professional gamblers and quantitative funds:

f* = (p × b − q) / b

Where: p = probability of winning
q = probability of losing = 1 − p
b = net odds received (decimal odds − 1)

Kelly sizing maximizes the long-term geometric growth rate of capital. In practice, a fractional Kelly (50% of the formula's recommended size) is used to account for model uncertainty, smoothing the variance while preserving the mathematical growth advantage.

Why This Market Is Exploitable

Sports books are not efficient markets in the academic sense. Several structural inefficiencies make them persistently exploitable:

  • Human oddsmaking: Unlike equity markets, odds are initially set by human analysts with known biases — recency bias, home team favoritism, public team overvaluation (teams like the Cowboys or Lakers are systematically overpriced because casual bettors like them).
  • Slow line movement: Once set, lines often move slowly, giving algorithmic systems time to identify and act on mispricings before the market corrects.
  • Cross-book inefficiency: Dozens of legal books operate with imperfect information sharing. Simultaneous price discrepancies are common and persistent enough to be systematically captured.
  • Prediction market correlation: Kalshi contracts on game outcomes and related propositions (e.g., "will a specific team cover the spread") provide additional pricing signals that can be cross-referenced against book lines to identify divergences.

By the law of large numbers, a strategy with positive expected value applied consistently across hundreds of independent bets will converge to its mathematical expectation. A single bet with +5% EV is a coin flip with a slight edge. One hundred such bets at appropriate Kelly sizing produces a near-certain profit over the sample.

Risk Management: Cross-Phase Architecture

Risk management is not phase-specific — it is a system-wide architectural principle. Several constraints apply across all three phases simultaneously:

  • Hard position limits: Phase 1 holds a maximum of 3 ETF positions. Phase 2 holds a maximum of 4 stock positions. Phase 3 (when active) will be capped at a defined percentage of total capital. No single event can concentrate the entire portfolio in one asset or outcome.
  • Automated stop-loss execution: All equity positions (Phases 1 and 2) use Alpaca bracket orders — a single API call that simultaneously places the entry order, a stop-loss sell order, and a take-profit sell order. The stop executes automatically without human intervention, even during premarket or after-hours gaps.
  • Real-time worst-case monitoring: The operating dashboard computes and displays the total dollar loss if every active position simultaneously hits its stop-loss. This number is visible at all times. On a $100,000 portfolio, this figure should never exceed $6,000–$8,000.
  • Capital segmentation: Each phase operates on a defined capital allocation. Phase 1 (ETF) targets 50–95% of equity capital depending on regime. Phase 2 (stocks) targets a separate allocation up to 64% (8 positions × 8% max notional). Phase 3 targets a defined pool independent of equity deployments. Losses in one phase do not cascade into others.

Infrastructure & Operations

The system is built entirely in Python, running on standard hardware with no proprietary data subscriptions required for Phases 1 and 2:

  • Market data: Yahoo Finance (yfinance) for historical and real-time price data — free, reliable, and sufficient for daily momentum strategies.
  • Order execution: Alpaca Markets API — a commission-free brokerage with a professional-grade REST API supporting bracket orders, paper trading mode, and real-time account monitoring.
  • Database: SQLite for trade logging, performance tracking, and portfolio snapshots. All trade history is preserved indefinitely.
  • Dashboard: Streamlit-based web interface showing live positions, P&L, regime indicators, ETF rankings, and the full stock screener output with research guides for each candidate.
  • Scheduler: Automated intraday checks for stop-loss events and rotation signals, running every 30 minutes during market hours.

The system is currently operating in paper trading mode on Alpaca's paper environment, which uses live market prices with simulated capital. This allows full validation of strategy behavior, execution logic, and risk management under real market conditions before live capital is deployed.

Path to Capital Deployment

The current pilot phase uses $100,000 in simulated paper trading capital across Phases 1 and 2. The validation criteria for transitioning to live capital:

  • Minimum 30 days of paper trading with behavior matching architectural design
  • At least one stop-loss event correctly executing (demonstrating risk management works)
  • Win rate and expectancy tracking at or above mathematical projections
  • Dashboard audit trail showing every trade decision, sizing calculation, and P&L outcome

Initial live deployment would begin with $2,000–$3,000 to validate execution mechanics under real fill conditions. If metrics hold over 60 days, scaling to the full available capital allocation follows using the same system without modification — the architecture is designed to scale linearly.
